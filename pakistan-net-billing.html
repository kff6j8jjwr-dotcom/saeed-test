Pakistan’s New Net Billing Policy: Why Solar Still Pays

By Saeed Rasheed

Pakistan’s recent shift from net metering to net billing has sparked widespread debate among solar consumers, investors, and policymakers. While the government has significantly reduced the export rate paid to solar users, solar economics remain strong because installation costs have fallen sharply while electricity tariffs remain high.

Major Policy Change: Lower Export Rates

Under the previous net-metering framework, consumers were compensated between Rs 19 and Rs 27 per unit exported to the grid. Under net billing, export compensation is approximately Rs 7 to Rs 9 per unit. The import tariff—what consumers pay for electricity from the grid—remains around Rs 60 to Rs 70 per unit.

The change shifts profitability away from exporting electricity and toward self-consumption, where consumers avoid buying expensive grid electricity.

A Typical 5 kW Household System

A standard 5 kW solar system generates around 3,600 units per year on average. Under the previous policy, annual savings were estimated at Rs 234,000, including self-use savings and export income. Under the new policy, the estimated annual benefit is Rs 166,800: Rs 156,000 from self-use and around Rs 10,800 from exports.

The saving is lower, but the payback period remains broadly stable at three to four years because installation costs have declined markedly.

Installation Costs Have Fallen

Three years ago, a 5 kW system in Pakistan cost about Rs 800,000, including roughly Rs 700,000 for equipment and Rs 100,000 for labour. Today, the same system costs around Rs 500,000, with equipment near Rs 400,000 and labour still around Rs 100,000. This lower entry cost improves investment returns despite reduced export income.

International Policy Alignment

The shift aligns Pakistan’s solar compensation structure with the United Kingdom, Germany, the United States, and Australia, where exported solar electricity is generally compensated at wholesale rather than retail rates. Globally, export compensation often ranges from 20 to 40 percent of retail prices. Pakistan’s new rate is lower, but its low installation costs and strong sunlight preserve attractive economics.

Solar Remains a Strong Investment

Solar remains financially attractive in Pakistan because of high electricity tariffs, lower installation costs, strong sunlight availability, and a short payback period. Consumers can obtain the strongest returns by maximising daytime consumption—running appliances, air conditioning, and business operations during solar hours.

Conclusion

Net billing marks a transition from export-driven solar profitability to savings driven by self-consumption. It does not end the case for solar; it changes the strategy. With lower upfront costs and expensive grid electricity, solar remains viable and economically compelling for many Pakistani consumers.
