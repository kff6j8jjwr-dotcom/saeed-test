Pakistan’s New Net Billing Policy: Why Solar Still Pays

By Saeed Rasheed

Pakistan’s New Net Metering Policy: Lower Export Rates, But Solar Remains Profitable

By Saeed Rasheed | Energy Policy Analysis

Pakistan’s recent shift from Net Metering to Net Billing has sparked widespread debate among solar consumers, investors, and policymakers. While the government has significantly reduced the export rate paid to solar users, the overall economics of solar investment remain strong due to a sharp decline in installation costs and high electricity tariffs.

This policy change reflects a global trend, aligning Pakistan’s solar compensation structure with countries such as the United Kingdom, Germany, the United States, and Australia, where exported solar electricity is compensated at wholesale rather than retail rates.

Major Policy Change: Export Rate Reduced by Nearly 60%

Under the previous net metering framework, solar consumers were compensated between Rs 19 and Rs 27 per unit for electricity exported to the national grid. Under the new net billing policy, export compensation has been reduced to approximately Rs 7 to Rs 9 per unit.

However, the import tariff—what consumers pay for electricity from the grid—remains unchanged at Rs 60 to Rs 70 per unit.

This change shifts solar profitability away from exporting electricity and toward self-consumption, where consumers avoid purchasing expensive grid electricity.

Solar Installation Costs Have Dropped 37%, Improving Investment Returns

Despite the reduction in export income, solar investment remains attractive due to a substantial decline in installation costs.

Three years ago, a 5-kW solar system in Pakistan cost approximately Rs 800,000, including Rs 700,000 in equipment and Rs 100,000 in Labor.

Today, the same system costs around Rs 500,000, with equipment costing Rs 400,000 and labour remaining at Rs 100,000.

In comparison, a similar system in the United Kingdom costs approximately Rs 1 million, primarily due to labour costs of Rs 400,000.

Payback Period Remains Stable at Approximately Three Years

Under the previous policy, the payback period was approximately 3.4 years.

Under the new policy, due to lower system costs, the payback period remains approximately 3-4 years.

This means solar remains a highly profitable investment in Pakistan despite the policy change.

Pakistan Aligns with Global Solar Compensation Models

Globally, solar export compensation ranges between 20 and 40 percent of retail electricity prices.

Pakistan’s previous policy offered export compensation equal to approximately 40 percent of retail tariffs, making it one of the most generous solar policies in the world.

Under the new policy, export compensation ranges between 12 and 17 percent of retail tariffs, slightly below the global average but still economically viable due to low installation costs.

Countries including the United Kingdom, Germany, the United States, and Australia have adopted similar net billing systems to maintain financial stability of electricity utilities.

Solar Remains a Strong Investment Opportunity

Despite reduced export compensation, solar power remains financially attractive in Pakistan due to:

High electricity tariffs

Low installation costs

Strong sunlight availability

Short payback period of approximately three years

Pakistan now offers one of the most favourable solar investment environments globally.

Conclusion: Shift Toward Self-Consumption Is the New Solar Strategy

The new net billing policy marks a transition from export-driven solar profitability to self-consumption-driven savings.

Consumers who maximize daytime use of solar electricity, running appliances, air conditioning, and business operations during solar hours, will continue to achieve strong financial returns.

Pakistan’s solar market is expected to continue expanding rapidly, with solar becoming an essential component of the country’s future energy mix.

Solar investment remains not only viable but economically compelling under the new policy framework.
