Free Food Culture & Self-Reliance in Karachi

By Saeed Rasheed

Free Food Culture and the Silent Decline of Self-Reliance in Karachi

Karachi, a city of resilience and hard work, is witnessing a rapidly expanding culture of free food distribution by various non-governmental organizations (NGOs). While humanitarian intentions behind these initiatives cannot be denied, it is time for a serious national conversation on whether unregulated free food programs are genuinely helping societyor slowly weakening its foundations.

Firstly, continuous free food distribution discourages the culture of hard work. When food is provided without any criteria, verification, or accountability, it unintentionally sends a message that effort and productivity are optional. Nations are not built on charity alone; they are built on dignity, Labor, and self-reliance.

Secondly, the downfall of nations historically begins with dependency and debt. Pakistan is already burdened with massive loans. Promoting a dependency mindset at the grassroots level only deepens this crisis. A population accustomed to free aid gradually loses motivation to contribute economically.

There are also serious violations of ethical and religious principles. Reports of slaughtering under-age goats (around six months old) for food distribution raise concerns, as this practice goes against accepted religious and legal standards. Charity must never compromise law or faith.

Instead of unconditional handouts, a subsidized food system would be far more effective. Even a minimal payment encourages self-respect and responsibility while still supporting those in need. Free distribution should be strictly limited to the disabled, elderly, widows, and those genuinely unable to earn.

e.g. DRSWA Daily several station where fresh ROTI and SALAN provided in very low price with the name SASSTA TANDOR

Alarmingly, many beneficiaries are already earning. Guards, labourers, and daily wage workers often earn at least Rs. 1,500 per day yet still avail free food. This misallocation deprives the truly deserving and reflects the absence of any eligibility checks.

Moreover, billions of rupees in Zakat and donations are collected annually without scrutiny of sources or transparent utilization. Charity funds must be regulated to ensure they are not misused, misdirected, or exploited for publicity rather than public welfare.

Imagine if these billions were invested in industries, vocational training, and small enterprises. Job creation is the most powerful form of helpit restores dignity, reduces crime, and strengthens the economy. Employment lasts longer than a meal.

Most concerning is the misuse of these facilities by drug addicts and habitual dependents, who repeatedly avail free food instead of seeking rehabilitation or employment. Charity without reform only sustains the problem.

In conclusion, compassion must be guided by wisdom. Karachi does not need an ever-growing charity cultureit needs structured support, accountability, and employment opportunities. Feeding the hungry is noble, but empowering the capable to stand on their own feet is nation-building.
