Middle East Strategy · 2025–2027

US–Israel Strategy: Perception vs Reality in the Middle East

By Saeed Rasheed

Introduction

Since mid-2025, global headlines have portrayed the United States and Israel as being drawn into an increasingly volatile Middle East conflict—reactive, overstretched, and facing uncertain outcomes. Media narratives frequently emphasise rising oil prices, regional instability, and the danger of escalation.

Beneath this surface-level interpretation lies a more complex possibility: recent developments may reflect not a reactive war, but a structured and long-term strategic shift in the region.

The Gap Between Narrative and Strategy

A dominant narrative suggests that the United States became involved without a clear plan. Yet several indicators point toward prior strategic alignment and phased execution: increasing intelligence coordination, pre-positioning of military assets, and strengthening of regional alliances. The events of 2025 may represent a trigger phase within a broader strategic framework rather than an unplanned escalation.

Iran and the Proxy Network

For decades, Iran’s regional influence has largely operated through allied non-state actors. Recent developments indicate that these networks have faced significant operational disruption. Leadership structures within groups such as Hamas and Hezbollah have reportedly been weakened, while communication and coordination capabilities appear to have been degraded. The threat has not disappeared, but its ability to function as a cohesive regional force may have been reduced.

Pressure on Strategic Capabilities

Attention has also focused on Iran’s strategic infrastructure. Reports suggest that elements of nuclear and missile programs have been targeted alongside individuals linked to advanced research and planning. It would be premature to conclude that these capabilities have been eliminated; however, evidence suggests they have been constrained and disrupted.

A Changing U.S. Role

The United States appears to be moving toward reduced troop deployment, greater reliance on intelligence and technological superiority, and increased use of naval and offshore capabilities. This approach seeks to minimise costs and casualties while maintaining strategic influence—a transition from direct intervention to indirect control and support.

Regional Self-Reliance and Energy Security

Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Turkey, and Pakistan are increasingly positioned as independent security actors, potentially forming new cooperative frameworks. Energy remains central: maintaining stable oil supply routes reinforces the importance of strategic stability in oil-producing regions. Though disruption in the Strait of Hormuz is often highlighted, international naval capabilities and economic interdependence make prolonged disruption unlikely.

Toward a New Strategic Order?

These developments may indicate reduced reliance on large-scale deployments, greater regional responsibility for security, continued emphasis on intelligence and technology, and efforts to contain conflict within manageable limits. Whether this produces lasting stability or new uncertainty remains open.

The events of 2025–2027 may be understood as instability and reactive conflict, or as a deliberate effort to reshape the regional strategic landscape. The truth likely lies somewhere in between, but the consequences will shape global geopolitics for years to come.
