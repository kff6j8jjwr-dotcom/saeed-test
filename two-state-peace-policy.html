Two-State Peace Policy: Recognition, Reality & Pakistan

By Saeed Rasheed

Introduction

The idea of a two-state agreement between Israel and Palestine is one of the most widely discussed solutions to the long-standing Middle East conflict. At its core, it envisages independent Israeli and Palestinian states, mutual recognition, and international acceptance and guarantees.

Can peace succeed if recognition is selective? If some countries recognise Israel but reject Palestine, or recognise Palestine but reject Israel, the foundation becomes weak. Durable peace requires mutual recognition between the parties and support from the international community.

What Does Two-State Truly Mean?

A true two-state solution requires Israel and Palestine to recognise each other’s sovereignty; borders to be negotiated, often from pre-1967 lines with adjustments; security guarantees for both sides; and international support for both states. Recognition cannot be one-sided.

Pakistan’s Historical Position

Pakistan has not recognised Israel, a position linked to support for Palestinian self-determination, religious and ideological solidarity, and statements from the early post-independence years. Pakistan’s founding leadership strongly supported the Palestinian cause, but the world of 1947 is not the world of today.

Changing Global Alliances

International relations evolve. The United States and Vietnam, the United States and Japan, and Egypt and Jordan with Israel show that former enemies can form diplomatic and strategic partnerships. Today’s world is multipolar and pragmatic: economic interests, technology, security, and regional stability often outweigh ideological positions.

Emotional Politics and Strategic Realism

Public feeling toward Palestine is deeply emotional, religious, historical, and symbolic. Yet long-term peace may require strategic patience, diplomatic engagement, gradual normalisation, and support for Palestinian state-building alongside dialogue with Israel.

Turkey, Egypt, Jordan, the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain, Morocco, and Sudan have taken steps to normalise relations with Israel, principally on strategic, economic, and security grounds.

Policy Choices for Pakistan

  • Status quo: continue non-recognition until Palestinian statehood is realised.
  • Conditional recognition: announce an intention to recognise Israel upon defined milestones toward Palestinian sovereignty.
  • Gradual engagement: begin backchannel diplomacy without formal recognition.
  • Full recognition with advocacy: establish relations while explicitly supporting a two-state solution.

Conclusion

The debate is not between loyalty and betrayal, but between policy strategies. One prioritises moral consistency and solidarity; the other strategic engagement and influence. Both seek peace and justice. Pakistan must balance national interest, regional stability, public opinion, and commitment to Palestinian rights. Foreign policy is not static: the question is how Pakistan chooses to respond to a changed world.
