Two-State Peace Policy: Recognition, Reality & Pakistan

By Saeed Rasheed

Two-State Agreement: Recognition, Reality, and the Changing World Order

Introduction

The idea of a two-state agreement between Israel and Palestine is one of the most widely discussed solutions to the long-standing conflict in the Middle East. At its core, the two-state solution envisions:

An independent State of Israel

An independent State of Palestine

Mutual recognition between both states

International acceptance and guarantees

However, a key question remains: Can peace succeed if recognition is selective? If some countries recognize Israel but reject Palestine, or recognize Palestine but reject Israel, the foundation of a two-state agreement becomes weak. Durable peace requires mutual recognition not only between the two parties but also from the international community.

What Does “Two-State” Truly Mean?

A true two-state solution means:

Israel and Palestine recognize each other’s sovereignty.

Borders are negotiated (often based on pre1967 lines with adjustments).

Security guarantees are provided to both sides.

The international community supports and recognizes both states.

Recognition cannot be onesided. If Muslim-majority countries refuse to recognize Israel, or if Israel’s allies reject Palestinian statehood, the agreement becomes symbolic rather than practical.

Why Do Some Countries Still Refuse Recognition?

Countries like Pakistan have not recognized Israel. Historically, this position has been linked to:

Support for Palestinian self-determination.

Religious and ideological solidarity.

Statements made during early postindependence years.

Pakistan’s founding leadership strongly supported the Palestinian cause. But the world of 1947 is not the world of today.

Changing Global Alliances: Lessons from History

International relations constantly evolve. Former enemies become allies; former allies become rivals.

Examples:

The United States and Vietnam fought a brutal war — today they have strong diplomatic and economic ties.

The United States and Japan were enemies in World War II — now they are strategic allies.

Germany and England now they are strategic allies

Afghanistan was a battlefield between the U.S. and Soviet Union; later, alliances shifted again.

Iran and Iraq fought an eight-year war — yet regional politics continue to reshape alignments.

Egypt, Jorden and Israel fought multiple wars — yet now maintain diplomatic relations.

The global system changed after:

World War I

World War II

The Cold War

Today’s world is multipolar and pragmatic. Economic interests, technology, security, and regional stability often outweigh ideological positions.

Emotional Politics vs Strategic Realism

Public opinion in many countries is deeply emotional regarding Palestine. The issue is not only political, it is religious, historical, and symbolic.

However, long-term peace may require:

Strategic patience

Diplomatic engagement

Gradual normalization

Support for Palestinian statebuilding alongside dialogue with Israel

The “new normal” in global politics is pragmatism. Countries increasingly prioritize:

Economic growth

Regional stability

Trade and technology partnerships

Muslim Countries That Recognized Israel

Several Muslim-majority countries have normalized relations with Israel:

Turkey (recognized Israel in 1949)

Egypt (1979 peace treaty)

Jordan (1994 peace treaty)

United Arab Emirates, Bahrain, Morocco, Sudan (2020 normalization steps)

These decisions were based largely on strategic, economic, and security interests rather than emotional rhetoric. They demonstrate that regional geopolitics has evolved.

Quaid-e-Azam’s Position and Historical Context

Pakistan’s founding father, Muhammad Ali Jinnah, strongly opposed the creation of Israel in 1947–48. At that time:

The Palestinian Arab leadership had rejected partition.

The Arab world was united against the plan.

The postcolonial Muslim world saw the issue as one of justice and displacement.

Jinnah reportedly described Israel as an unjust imposition in that era’s context. However, that was 1947, a period immediately after colonial withdrawal and global war.

The geopolitical environment of today is vastly different:

Cold War alignments have ended.

Former enemies (U.S.–Vietnam, Japan–U.S., Egypt–Israel) are now partners.

Strategic alliances shift based on economic and security interests.

Background

The 1947 UN Partition Plan proposed two states. War prevented implementation. Subsequent conflicts reshaped territory and hardened positions.

Pakistan has historically opposed recognition of Israel, aligning with Palestinian self-determination and reflecting the position of Muhammad Ali Jinnah.

However, geopolitical conditions have evolved significantly.

Argument for Recognition

Geopolitical RealityIsrael is internationally recognized and integrated into global institutions.

Muslim PrecedentCountries like Turkey, Egypt, Jordan, and Gulf states maintain relations.

Strategic LeverageEngagement may allow Pakistan to influence peace initiatives.

Economic & Technological BenefitsCooperation in agriculture, water management, cybersecurity, and innovation could be beneficial.

Diplomacy Over IsolationRecognition can coexist with continued advocacy for Palestinian rights.

Argument Against Recognition

Unresolved Palestinian StatehoodRecognition without a sovereign Palestinian state may weaken Pakistan’s moral stance.

Domestic Political SensitivityPublic opinion strongly supports Palestine.

Regional Security ConcernsThe conflict remains volatile with actors like Hamas and Hezbollah involved in recurring violence.

Conditional Diplomacy ApproachSome argue recognition should only follow a finalized twostate settlement.

Policy Options for Pakistan

Status Quo – Continue non-recognition until Palestinian statehood is realized.

Conditional Recognition – Announce intent to recognize Israel upon specific milestones toward Palestinian sovereignty.

Gradual Engagement – Begin backchannel diplomacy without formal recognition.

Full Recognition with Advocacy – Establish diplomatic relations while explicitly supporting a two-state solution.

Conclusion

The debate is not between loyalty and betrayal — it is between two policy strategies.

One prioritizes moral consistency and solidarity.

The other prioritizes strategic engagement and influence.

Both seek peace and justice.

The ultimate decision for Pakistan must balance:

National interest

Regional stability

Public opinion

Commitment to Palestinian rights

Foreign policy is not static. It evolves with global realities. The question is not whether the world has changed — it clearly has. The question is how Pakistan chooses to respond.
